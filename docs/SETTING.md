# Setting and story: working notes

First collected 2026-11-05. These are ideas and leanings, not decisions. Story, lore and the main quest are parked while the project works on the world itself (see `CLAUDE.md`). This file is the reference for when they come back.

## The central theme: everything at once

The strongest idea so far is internal contradiction: many movements and developments happening *at the same time*. Science sits next to alchemy, merchants next to nobles, learning next to religious revival, and early machines next to medieval stone. The setting doesn't sort these into a single line of progress. They coexist, clash and borrow from each other.

Most of the sections below describe one side of such a pair.

## Period and inspiration

- Broadly the early modern period, centred on the 17th century.
- The world is its own: a wild mix of things European from that time. The strongest flavours are Dutch, English and 16th-century Italian.
- The Elder Scrolls is not an inspiration for the setting. Everything from it that is in the game now will be replaced: the names, gods and races, the guilds, and the premise of the ghost king. There will be no Mages Guild.
- *Why* this period appeals hasn't been written down yet. That was left open on purpose.

## Where differences come from

- There are no strong divisions between polities.
- Variety comes instead from going from more rural areas to more urbanised ones.
- Where polities do exist, cultural boundaries don't follow their borders.

## Discovery and knowledge

- The culture puts a high value on discovery, finding universal truths, experiment and science.
- It does so in a way that isn't strictly modern. Natural philosophy easily blurs into alchemy, and the people of the setting wouldn't draw a sharp line between the two.
- Humanist leanings belong here as well.
- Some clerics are scientists themselves, while the church may be at war with a figure like Galileo or Bruno. The church is on both sides of the question.

## Commerce

- There is a strong commercial culture. This is where the Dutch and English influences are strongest.
- Society is largely proto-capitalist or capitalist, or at least it has a commercial elite.
- That commercial elite is only a small part of the elite as a whole.

## Nobility and the Baroque

- There is a nobility.
- At least part of it shows its power through Baroque architecture and the philosophy that goes with it.

## Faith: the sublime, revival and radicalism

- The search for knowledge and the humanist tendencies are set against the period's strong sense of the sublime. Put more sharply, they are set against its religious revivalism and radicalism.
- The Baroque grew out of the Counter-Reformation and emphasised the larger-than-life nature of God. The setting should have this. *(An earlier conversation about this is still to be linked here.)*
- As a result, the Baroque carries two meanings in the setting. In the church it speaks of the greatness of God, and in the noble houses it speaks of their own power.
- Religious movements inspired by the Puritans, Lutherans and Calvinists provide a contrast.
- Because the world is its own, the faiths are invented ones modelled on these real movements rather than the real churches by name.

## Technology

- There are very early versions of later technology.
- **The train** is short. It runs from one part of the city to another, or from the city to somewhere a little way outside it. It is never long-distance travel.
- It is modelled on the very first experiments with rails and steam, not on the railway as it later became. The picture to aim for is a wagon on rails with a strange new engine on it.
- Rails belong to mining, as the historical wagonways did, and the two can be combined. For example, a mine or quarry line could be where the new engine is tried out.

### Historical reference points

- **Rails came before engines.** Wagonways were wooden rails with horse-drawn or hand-pushed wagons:
  - Agricola's *De re metallica* (1556) shows carts on plank tracks in German mines.
  - Huntingdon Beaumont's Wollaton Wagonway near Nottingham (1603–04) carried coal about 3 km from the pits.
- **17th-century steam was for pumping water and for marvels.** There was no transport yet:
  - Giovanni Branca's steam-jet wheel (1629).
  - The Marquess of Worcester's "water-commanding engine" (1660s).
  - Denis Papin's experiments with a pressure cooker (his "digester") and a piston (1679–1690).
  - Savery's mine pump (1698), then Newcomen's engine (1712).
- **A cleric's steam cart.** Around 1672 Ferdinand Verbiest, a Jesuit at the Chinese court, described, and possibly built, a small steam-driven cart as a curiosity for the emperor. This fits the cleric-scientist idea.
- **Steam as a spectacle.** Much later, in 1808, Trevithick's "Catch Me Who Can" ran on a circular track in London as a paid novelty ride.

## The world as it stands

- What's in the game now leans medieval, and that's fine.
- On 2026-11-12 every Elder Scrolls name and term was replaced with a stand-in, chosen to fit these notes rather than as decisions:
  - **Places:** Daggerfall became **Aldhaven**, a port on **Merrow Bay**; Vellaro and Brakkenburg are distant cities in rumours.
  - **Faith:** the Temple of Arkay became the **Church of Saint Hallam**, with its rector and churchyard.
  - **Learning and arms:** the Mages Guild became the **Philosophers' Society**, whose "spells" are its arts and whose magicka is **spirit**; the Fighters Guild became the **Fencing School**.
  - **The parked story:** the Emperor became the **Stadtholder**, King Lysandus **King Aldwin** (the ghost), King Gothryd **King Ottmar**, Queen Aubk-i **Queen Isabeau**, the court mage a **court philosopher**, the necromancer **Corvin Ash**, and the soul gem a **black stone**.
  - **Origins** replace the races: Townborn, Marcher, Highlander, Portborn, Courtborn, Forester, Wanderer and Fenlander, which follows the rural-to-urban idea above.
  - **Other:** weapon materials run Iron, Steel, Silver, Blued, Damascus, Blackened, Meteoric. The calendar is ordinary months and weekdays in the year 1652, under one moon. People's names are Dutch, English and Italian.
- Old, outdated architecture standing around everywhere is a good thing. It gives the setting texture, and it's one of the "at the same time" layers in its own right.
- A new game already starts on a lookout called Pilgrim's Rise.

## The story: a pilgrimage

- The protagonist is a religious person on a pilgrimage.
- On the way, the pilgrim runs into contradictions within the faith and into the many different ways others interpret it. Whether the pilgrim agrees with any of these is up to the player.
- The goal is a newly discovered holy place in a New World, an analogue of the Americas.
- That place should be truly fantastic, sublime and divine, in contrast to the banal and grounded nature of the rest of the game. A point of comparison is Disco Elysium, whose very realistic world ends in something beyond the natural.

## Open questions

- **Why this period:** the reasons, whenever you want to write them down.
- **Magic:** with no Mages Guild, is there anything supernatural outside the holy place? Or is alchemy simply the science of the day, whether or not it works?
- **The pilgrim:** is this a fixed character, or someone the player creates? (Character creation is currently parked behind the combat setting.) And which faith does the pilgrim belong to: the Baroque church, one of the reformed movements, or something in between?
- **The New World:** is the holy place inhabited, and if so by whom? An analogue of the Americas brings conquest, colonisation and the people who already live there along with it, so the story needs a stance on that.
- **Getting there:** how much of the game takes place in the New World, and how does the pilgrim travel there? A sea voyage from the port city is one possibility.
- **The train's builder:** was it built by merchants, by a noble, by a cleric-scientist, or by the mine owners?
